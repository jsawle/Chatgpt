Teaching Epicentre Lab

Learning sequence

  1. Watch P arrive before S at the three stations.
  2. Pick the first small signal (P) and larger signal (S). Compare S–P gaps.
  3. Show one distance sphere, then two, then three. A station measures distance, not direction.
  4. Calculate the underground focus from the sphere intersections. Reveal to check its location and depth.
  5. Add S–P timing uncertainty and discuss why real distance constraints may not meet exactly.

Names and data

West Ridge, North Peak, South Shore and the additional East Point timing reference are invented station names at the same coordinates as the first prototype: 0° E, 40° N; 15° E, 47° N; and 18° E, 32° N. They are not real monitoring stations. All earthquake events, traces and arrival times are synthetic.

Scientific scope

The epicentre is the surface location above the underground origin, or hypocentre. This version solves latitude, longitude and depth using three straight-line distances in a homogeneous spherical Earth. The circle method is commonly called triangulation; distance-based location is more precisely trilateration.

The model uses Earth-centred Cartesian chord distance from the underground focus to each surface station. Three distance spheres yield two mathematical candidates; the app uses a fourth synthetic S–P observation from East Point (30° E, 40° N) to select the better-fitting interior candidate within the 0–700 km teaching range, or reports missing evidence. The fourth timing is entered numerically; no fourth trace is shown. This is a noiseless geometric teaching solver, not a real seismic locator. P speed is 6 km/s and S speed is 3.5 km/s, illustrative constants rather than a universal velocity model. Real waves travel through the interior, refract and depend on depth and Earth's structure. Wireframe spherical fronts expand from the focus in 3D. Dots travel along direct interior rays. The homogeneous model omits refraction, reflection, attenuation and core effects; fronts continuing outside the surface are mathematical extensions, not waves travelling through air.

tP = d/vP and tS = d/vS. Subtracting removes an unknown common origin-time offset: S–P = d(1/vS − 1/vP). Thus d = (S–P)/(1/vS − 1/vP). The uncertainty slider applies to the gap, not separately to each pick. Dashed inner and outer spheres show distance bounds; the solver does not propagate these bounds into a depth confidence interval.

The timeline uses seconds after the invented event for convenience. Real S–P measurement does not require knowing the event start time. Trace amplitude and frequency are illustrative and do not indicate magnitude. Model picks are an optional answer aid. Challenge mode hides the source and travelling rings until reveal.

How the calculation works

  1. Subtract P arrival from S arrival to remove the common origin-time offset.
  2. Multiply each gap by 8.4 km/s to obtain a straight-line distance in this constant-speed model.
  3. Each distance defines a sphere centred on its station. Solve the three sphere equations |x − station|² = distance² to find two candidate Cartesian positions.
  4. Compare the candidates with the fourth East Point distance and the allowed interior depth range.
  5. Convert the selected Cartesian position to latitude and longitude. Depth = 6371 km minus the distance of the focus from Earth’s centre. Project radially to the surface to mark the epicentre.

Small timing errors can produce large depth errors. A zero residual for three spheres is not proof of an accurate real-world depth. This solver selects a candidate; it does not perform a full noisy-data least-squares fit or calculate a confidence interval.

Depth in real seismology

Real location methods fit arrival times using Earth velocity models and more observations. Depth phases such as pP, a P wave reflected from the surface near the source, provide additional depth evidence. They are explained here but not simulated.
