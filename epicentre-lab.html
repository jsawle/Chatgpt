Teaching Epicentre Lab

Learning sequence

  1. Predict which wave arrives first. Play and compare the stations.
  2. Measure the P and S arrival times. Explain why the gap grows with distance.
  3. Enable station A, then B, then C. Discuss the possible locations remaining.
  4. Click the map to estimate the epicentre. Reveal and compare.
  5. Add timing uncertainty. Explain why real locations have uncertainty.

Science and limitations

The epicentre is the surface location above the earthquake's underground origin (hypocentre). This prototype models surface distance only; it does not solve focal depth. Classroom circle location is often called triangulation; distance-based location is more precisely trilateration.

All traces, arrival times and earthquake scenarios are synthetic. Constant speeds of 6 and 3.5 km/s are illustrative model parameters, not a universal Earth velocity model. Distance is a spherical great-circle distance. The same distance is used consistently to generate arrivals and draw circles. Real P and S waves travel through Earth's interior; the animated rings are only a visual analogy for their modelled progress.

In this model, tP = d/vP and tS = d/vS. Subtracting removes the unknown origin-time offset: Δt = d(1/vS − 1/vP). Therefore d = Δt/(1/vS − 1/vP). Actual earthquake location uses travel-time models, depth and many observations; circles need not meet exactly. USGS travel-time tables are model- and depth-dependent.

The timeline starts at the event for teaching convenience. In practice, the S–P difference can be measured without knowing when the event began. Amplitude, frequency and decay of these traces are invented to make the arrivals readable; they do not represent magnitude, site effects or real instrument response.

Teacher prompts

  • Does a station tell us direction or only distance?
  • Why does the closest station have the smallest S–P gap?
  • Why are three well-spaced stations more useful than one?
  • Why is an epicentre not the same as a hypocentre?
  • Which assumptions could make our location wrong?

Suggested audience: guided grades 6–8; model critique and algebra for grades 9–12. Younger learners can use teacher-led playback.
